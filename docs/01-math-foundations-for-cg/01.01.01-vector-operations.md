# Vector Operations

## Vector Addition

Geometrically adding two vectors is quite simple. The vector $\mathbf{c}$ is the sum of vector $\mathbf{a}$ and vector $\mathbf{b}$.


$$
\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}
$$

We can construct vector $\mathbf{c}$ by 'displacing' vector $\mathbf{b}$ to 'start' at the head of $\mathbf{a}$. By displacing the vector, we aren't changing any of its properties, since as mentioned in the [previous chapter](01.01-vectors), a vector's properties does not hold a location.

Performing this step gives us vector $\mathbf{b'}$. Next we just create a new vector starting at the tail of vector $\mathbf{a}$ and ending at the head of the displaced vector $\mathbf{b'}$, and that gives us vector $\mathbf{c}$.

The method of adding two vectors in this fashion is also commonly referred to as the Head-to-Tail Rule.

To take it a step further, we could have also said:

$$
\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}
$$

But when we apply the same steps as before for vector addition, we see that we get the same result. We can say that the order in which we add two vectors together does not matter, vector addition has the commutative property.

$$
\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}
$$

Adding two vectors algebraically is incredibly simple. To add two vectors, you simply add their corresponding components together.

$$
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (a_x + b_x,\ a_y + b_y,\ a_z + b_z)
$$

**Example:** take the same $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ from the diagrams above, $\mathbf{a} = (2, 0, -3)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (1.5, 0, 2)$.

$$
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (2 + 1.5,\ 0 + 0,\ -3 + 2) = (3.5, 0, -1)
$$

Which is exactly $\mathbf{c}$, matching the geometric construction. This works the same way no matter how many dimensions the vectors have:

$$
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (a_1 + b_1, a_2 + b_2, ..., a_n + b_n)
$$
