# Vectors

The [previous chapter](./03-linear-algebra) already slightly talked about vectors without really defining one, using them to represent positions and directions. This chapter slows down and looks at what a vector actually is.

## What is a vector?

Geometrically, a vector is an arrow: it has a magnitude (length) and a direction, and nothing else. If we draw a hundred arrows anywhere in space, as long as they all share the same length and the same direction, they're all the same vector.

This property, having no fixed position of its own, only a length and a direction, is why these are called **free vectors**.

Algebraically, a vector is a list of numbers, called its components. That list can hold any number of components, but since we will mostly be working in two or three dimensions, we'll keep it to two and three dimensional vectors. Below is the three-dimensional vector $\mathbf{v}$ with components $3$, $6$, and $2$:

$$
\mathbf{v} = (3, 6, 2)
$$

:::info Notation
Vectors are also commonly written with an arrow instead of bold, $\vec{v}$ instead of $\mathbf{v}$. Both mean the same thing, bold will be used throughout for consistency.
:::

Plotted in three-dimensional space, its three components are just distances measured along the x-, y-, and z-axes. Meaning we move $3$ units on the x-axis, $6$ units on the y-axis, and $2$ units on the z axis.

That's the same vector described two ways, geometrically as an arrow, algebraically as its components:

$$
\mathbf{v} = (v_x, v_y, v_z)
$$

Both descriptions are the same object, you'll switch between them depending on what's convenient. The geometric picture is useful for reasoning and visualizing, the algebraic one is what actually gets typed into code.

## A vector is not a point

A lot of people think points and vectors are the same thing, they are **not**. But it's understandable where the confusion comes from: any point can be tied to a unique vector, the one that starts at the origin $(0, 0, 0)$ and ends at that point. This is called a **position vector**, and its components are just the point's coordinates, that's why we end up writing both a point and a vector as `(x, y, z)`. A position vector isn't a special kind of vector though, it's just an ordinary free vector like any other. We've simply agreed to always draw it starting at the origin, so it can stand in for "where a point is" instead of just a magnitude and direction. The vector itself doesn't know it's being used that way, it's still the same object with no location of its own. A point is a specific place in space.

The distinction matters because the two don't support the same operations, because they are not the same. Vector operations will be explained further in the [next chapter](./03.01.01-basic-vector-operations).

The two do go hand in hand however. Given 2 points we can construct a vector, or given a point and a vector, we can move the point to a new position. But again, this is explained in the [next chapter](./03.01.01-basic-vector-operations), and will be revisited in chapter [4. Transformations](./04-transformations).

## Magnitude

As said before, a vector is defined as a direction and a magnitude. And we've somewhat seen the direction of a vector when plotting it in a three dimensional space, but how can we get the magnitude of a vector?

:::info Terminology
The magnitude of a vector is often also described as its length or its norm. Both of these terms mean the same thing as a vectors magnitude. Going forward, these terms will be used interchangeably.
:::

You may remember the Pythagorean theorem from school, $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, for finding the length of a right triangle's hypotenuse. The magnitude of a vector is the distance from its tail to its tip, and getting it turns out to be that exact same theorem, just extended to as many dimensions as you need:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2}
$$

Where $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert$ is the notation for the length of a vector $\mathbf{v}$.

<Proof title="(extra) Where the magnitude formula comes from">

The Pythagorean theorem shows us that:

$$
\mathbf{c}^2 = {a^2 + b^2}
$$

Where $c$ is the length of the hypotenuse of a right triangle, and $a$ and $b$ are the lengths of its other sides.

Now, given a vector $\mathbf{v} = (v_x, v_y)$, we can construct a right triangle from that where $\mathbf{v}$ is the hypotenuse:

This leans if we apply the Pythagorean theorem we get:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2
$$

Which then becomes:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}
$$

Now add a third dimension. Picture $\mathbf{v} = (v_x, v_y, v_z)$ as the diagonal of a box. First find the length of its shadow in the $xy$-plane, that's just the 2D case above:

$$
d = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}
$$

That diagonal $\mathbf{d}$ and the component $\mathbf{v_z}$ form a second right triangle, this time with $\mathbf{v}$ itself as the hypotenuse. It may not look like $\mathbf{v}$ is the hypotenuse, since it doesn't link up with its other sides. But remember, vectors don't have fixed positions, we can mentally slide $\mathbf{d}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{v_z}$ to form our right triangle. And then we can apply the Pythagorean theorem again to get:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{d^2 + v_z^2} = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2}
$$

The same trick keeps working no matter how many dimensions you add, which is why the formula generalizes so cleanly to:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + ... + v_n^2}
$$

Where n is the amount of dimensions in your vector.

</Proof>

**Example:** take $\mathbf{v} = (3, 4, 12)$.

$$
\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13
$$


## Special Vectors

### Zero Vector

The vector $\mathbf{v} = (0, 0, 0)$ is called the **zero vector**. Its magnitude is $0$, and since it has no length, it has no defined direction either, the one exception to a vector always being a magnitude *and* a direction.

### Unit Vector

Any vector with magnitude $1$ is called a **unit vector**. They show up everywhere since they isolate pure direction, with the scale factored out.

A unit vector is commonly written with a hat instead of the usual bold or arrow, $\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ instead of $\mathbf{v}$, to make it clear at a glance that it's a unit vector.

You'll see how to construct one, called *normalizing* a vector, in the [next chapter](./03.01.01-basic-vector-operations).

### Standard Basis Vectors

Each axis has its own unit vector pointing along it, called the **standard basis vectors**, written with hats just like any other unit vector:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{i}} = (1, 0, 0), \quad \hat{\mathbf{j}} = (0, 1, 0), \quad \hat{\mathbf{k}} = (0, 0, 1)
$$

You'll see how these are used to break any other vector down into its components in the [next chapter](./03.01.01-basic-vector-operations).
