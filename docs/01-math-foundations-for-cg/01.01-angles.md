# Angles

## Definition

An angle is formed between two lines, or rays, that meet at a shared point, its vertex. Officially, it's defined as:

$$
\alpha = \frac{l}{r}
$$

Where $\alpha$ is the angle itself, $l$ is the length of the arc that lies on a circle with center $O$ and radius $r$.

This ratio doesn't depend on which circle you draw it on. Change the circle's radius, and the arc grows or shrinks by that same factor, so $l/r$ comes out identical either way.

## Units

The definition of an angle divides one length by another, so the unit of length cancels out, leaving us with a dimensionless number. That number is given its own unit, the **radian**, to make it clear it's an angle being measured and not just any ratio.

**Example:** take an arc of length $l = 6$ on a circle of radius $r = 5$:

$$
\alpha = \frac{l}{r} = \frac{6}{5} = 1.2 \text{ radians}
$$

People are often more familiar with another unit for angles, **degrees**. Degrees are just a different way of expressing the same angle, and converting between degrees and radians is straightforward if you know how they relate. A full circle measures $360°$, but how many radians is that?

Using the definition $\alpha = l/r$: going all the way around a circle means the arc $l$ equals the full circumference, $2\pi r$, so:

$$
\alpha_{\text{full circle}} = \frac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi \text{ radians}
$$

That gives us a single relationship between degrees and radians:

$$
360° = 2\pi \text{ radians}
$$

which is all we need to convert between the two:

$$
1° = \frac{2\pi}{360} = \frac{\pi}{180} \text{ radians}, \qquad 1 \text{ radian} = \frac{360}{2\pi} = \frac{180}{\pi}°
$$

:::tip Converting between degrees and radians
- Degrees -> radians: multiply by $\pi/180$
- Radians -> degrees: multiply by $180/\pi$
:::

**Example:** convert $90°$ to radians:

$$
90° \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{2} \text{ radians}
$$

It's important that you know how to convert between the two: functions like $\sin$ and $\cos$, and most math libraries, expect their input in radians, so plugging in a value that's actually in degrees will silently produce a completely wrong result.

## Common Angles

A handful of angles come up often enough that it's worth knowing their radian equivalents by heart, having them on hand makes converting, reasoning, and testing much quicker:

| Degrees | Radians |
| --- | --- |
| $0°$ | $0$ |
| $30°$ | $\pi/6$ |
| $45°$ | $\pi/4$ |
| $60°$ | $\pi/3$ |
| $90°$ | $\pi/2$ |
| $180°$ | $\pi$ |
| $360°$ | $2\pi$ |

:::info
This table goes further than this, once you look at the [angle pairs](#angle-pairs) (which are mentioned further down). If you know $30° = \pi/6$, you basically already know its supplementary angle ($150° = 180° - 30° = \pi - \pi/6 = 5\pi/6$), its coterminal angles, and more, for free.
:::

## Signed Orientation

So far, an angle has only measured a positive size. But an angle can also carry a sign, depending on which direction you rotate from one arm to the other.

By convention, rotating **counterclockwise** (CCW) is positive, and rotating **clockwise** (CW) is negative. So $+90°$ turns a quarter-turn counterclockwise, while $-90°$ turns a quarter-turn clockwise, landing somewhere different even though both angles are the same *size*:
